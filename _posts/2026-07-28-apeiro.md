title: "Apeiro"
date: 2026-07-28
category: poetry
year: freshman
---

I was on the treadmill: I don’t know how long I walked. But I didn’t feel tired at all when I finally got off.

It was midnight then, I suppose. The machine slowly came to a stop. My feet were planted on the conveyor and my hair was matted with sweat. My hands gripped the machine’s arms.

I stared at the wall, stared with that blank gaze. I didn’t have questions nor concerns. Neither did the wall before me. It was a nice wall, a kind wall: it didn’t judge. Maybe it did, actually. After all, it stood watch over me every single night, watching me recollect like this. But it had no will to voice these judgments — or perhaps it had no mouth — so I was content.

Some beeping suddenly sputtered from below, and I quietly realized it was the machine. The screen blinked and some numbers appeared. There were a lot of them, a long string of meaningless tones, each worth more to the next person than myself. I gave them a short glance, and dully powered off the machine. Darkness engulfed the room, the only source of light gone.

Fine in the dark, I walked to my bed and lay upon it, and stared at the black ceiling, all coated in nothing as it was — made of nothing I, at times, thought. What an existence that would be, that is, to not exist.

I had scarcely more than thought this when a faint clanking, as of some glass bottle falling, roused the ceiling from its nothing sleep. At that fallen tone, a voice remotely feminine whispered from above in such a sonorous voice it must have yelled: “This is the last time, Greg…The last! You’re such a fucking piece of shit! I’m gone, Greg, I’m gone!” And I suppose that is when the glass bottle shattered into a million unrecoverable pieces; but the next morning those shards were always fixed back together again. She always came back. Always.

And every time she did — returning to his doorstep, entering inside, and seemingly making up with him for some innumerable-th time — the ceiling would shake rhythmically. I took no interest in my nothing ceiling then. I took no interest in anything then.

That was them, though, and I was me. What they did was not my problem — legally speaking. Morally, maybe. But at times these bouts angered me. I didn’t rage nor crash into what little furniture there was in my apartment, but I certainly grew something more than annoyed. I was surely justified in this feeling, for I’m sure anyone would agree their heads looked better on pikes. Perhaps then they would finally shut the fuck up. I prayed to my nothing ceiling, which — on this particular night — seemed as infinite as the span of time that stretched from when I mounted the treadmill to when I was removed from it.

As the matter consumed my thoughts, so did sleep consume them; and soon I was snoring soundly in the night.

The moon paced outside, though its light never penetrated the blinds, for there were no blinds. I lived not on the ground floor, but below — and they did not know. Their minds were none the wiser. And so the moon’s dull rays merely illuminated that no man’s land which I dare not venture into. I dare not, for the last time I did no good thing came from it. I wake, eat, exercise, sleep. This is how it is. This is how it was; how it always has been, and how it always will be.

The early rustling of my formerly nothing ceiling betrayed that dawn had fallen outside, had bested night and awoken. My eyelids gently cracked and allowed the faint dim light that broke from some place — oh how I would like to know where! — to peer in at me and illuminate the room. I got out of bed, stretched, and sighed.

As was usual for morning, I opened the door and went upstairs. I moved cautiously when I reached the highest stair. Peering around the doorway to check if they had left the blinds down, I noticed that they had. No one was home, either. They both worked in the office. I think it was a long commute: they were never here in the morning. So I went to the refrigerator and set the door slightly ajar; the faint light emanated as of some treasure inside. I could smell my own hunger.

Yesterday, in particular, had been terribly unpleasant — he had stayed up watching the game and run out of beer. He was worst sober. I found no chance to acquire food then. So I made haste now, eating my fill.

Eggs, I ate those raw. Apples and carrots, I shoved down my throat. Beef, raw as when it was slain, with gore dripping down its tender portions, I ruthlessly tore into. My teeth gorged the food. I reached into a cabinet and procured a bottle of wine which I drank dropless. I licked my fingers, which had turned an odd mixture of purple and red, curtesy of blood and wine. I stopped for a moment. Would they notice the wine? If she returned first, wine would be her first lover; and his, vice-versa. They would notice the missing bottle. It wasn’t cheap wine, either. He was more of a beer guy.

However, I can say without doubt that she would still accuse him of drinking it, regardless of whether he denies it or not; the truth is immaterial in fragmented relationships. I’ve learned that through audio alone.

I was safe.

Of course, I cleaned what I had dirtied, washed my hands, and threw out what I had used. Precautions were still necessary.

I was in the middle of washing a napkin I had used to mop up a spill when the front door opened wide. I didn’t hear it: the faucet was running loudly. Light steps, two sets of them, poured in. It was her and another.

“Is that the faucet? God, the water bill! Can you go and turn it off for me, sweetie?”

The footsteps died and started out again. She had stayed behind for a moment. Her kid went ahead. I saw him first. He looked to me an illusion — for what was this? Who would be here now? Then I recalled the weight of his steps, the sound of those steps, and understood. He saw maybe a flutter of some quick, imperceptible thing as I dove over the counter and rolled away. I knocked the wine bottle — which I had yet to throw away — off the counter. I threw myself back against the wall, gaze wavering and totally frightened. I scrambled into the stairwell, then tumbled down the stairs and flung myself into my room. The kid, carefree, waddled in moments after and turned off the faucet. She came in a little later, saw the glass shards of the broken wine bottle, and scolded the kid for having destroyed it. The kid denied the accusation. But kids lie. Kids lie. Frustrated, she relented and dropped the matter.

Back pressed against the door, I slid against it to the ground. I had never been so close to discovery, so close to death! They would kill me if they knew, kill me a million times over. I was better off here, in my little apartment. I paid! I paid through chores. I cleaned; swept, at times. I had earned my little apartment. What right had they to steal it?

Oh, God…again, again! The treadmill! The treadmill!

I was running before I realized. Running, wheezing; but my heart never reached the pace it had earlier. It never beat so hard, but it beat to the pace of the running rubber that slipped back beneath my feet. Back, and around again. I cannot say when first I regained consciousness, though I can say that it has been far from the ruined mood that wracked me before. I thanked the treadmill with my silent steps for robbing me of despair.

It has been a while now, I think. I should stop; I shouldn’t tire myself. But I didn’t stop, and kept running. Not harder, just something between a light jog and a starting run: the sort of pace that sobered me, that robbed me off my constant delirium — the sort of uneven rhythm that kept me lucid.
