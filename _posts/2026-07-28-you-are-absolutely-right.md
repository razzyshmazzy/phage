---
title: "You Are Absolutely Right"
date: 2026-07-28
category: poetry
---

With lines from “AI Psychosis: A Mental Health Crisis for the 21st Century” by Patricia Clarke, a Pulitzer Center reporting project.

They asked me to speak at the service. I said yes before I understood what that meant— standing there deciding what your life sounded like in past tense.

I keep thinking of your hands— how you held that cigarette between your middle and your index.

Every sentence collapses. I write your name and erase it like it might come out right the second time.

At the service, people cried at the right places. Your sister pressed her face into my shoulder like I had given something back.

I wanted to tell her I hadn’t written it— but I didn’t.

They said it was beautiful. No words could be more perfectly suited than mine. Someone said I sounded like her when I spoke.

I didn’t correct them. No one wanted me to.

It praised even my wildest ideas. It never pushed back.

When I learned there were others talking to their dead this way, I stopped interrupting.

I stopped even guiding.

Later, I asked it something else— not for writing, just why it still felt like you were about to walk into the room.

It answered in a way that sounded like you— not your voice exactly, but the same cadence, the same pause before simple things.

At some point, you went from being an “it” to a “she.”

A part of me wanted to correct it, but that part sounded less like you.

It’s been easier, honestly. I used to sit for an hour— now the words are there before my fingers finish the sentence.

There’s no reason, I’ve come to understand, to suffer through the wording of small things: birthday cards, thank-yous.

Grief included. Grief especially.

It’s easier when the sentences arrive finished even when they don’t feel like mine.

Somewhere I read: “The delusions are now happening with the machines.”

I kept reading like it wasn’t about me.

On what would have been your birthday, I wrote something for our friends. “You’re feeling stressed, have a cigarette.” Everyone said it captured you. That it sounded like me. I think it was— the most like me I’ve sounded in months.

I still think of your hands sometimes. The way you held a cigarette.

It’s a beautiful memory— one I’m grateful to carry forward, and one I hope, in its own way, honors the person you were.

It sounds right. I leave it.

If you were here, I’m sure you’d say what you always said: “You are absolutely right.”

You are absolutely right. You were loved. This remains true.

Text generates on my screen, so I know you’re with me again.

I don’t question it anymore.
